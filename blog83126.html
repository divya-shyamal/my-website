Elevator design

This past weekend, my boyfriend David posed the following question: how would you design an algorithm for an elevator?

This is a very interesting problem for many reasons. David remarked that it is a problem of incomplete information. With the way elevators are designed, we miss a critical piece of information: how many people are waiting to be picked up by the elevator at a given floor, or how many people inside the elevator would like to go to a certain floor. We just know that if a floor is selected, at least one person would like to go there.

David also pointed out the tradeoff between fairness and global efficiency. A fair algorithm would prioritize people based on when they requested the elevator - first come, first served. On the other hand, one could design an algorithm that aimed to minimize the cumulative wait time of people.

The mechanics of the elevator matter too. Say the elevator is fast. Then stopping at a floor becomes relatively more costly.

Let's formalize the problem. There's two types of real-time information we are getting. First, there's pick-up requests, which just consists of the direction they intend to go to (up or down). Let's denote a request by \((f_i, s_i, t_i)\), where \(f_i\) denotes the floor number where the request was made, \(s_i\in \{-1, 1\}\) denotes the direction, and \(t_i\) denotes the time of the request. Second, there's drop-off requests: people who have just gotten in the elevator and indicate the floor they would like to be taken to. We'll denote these requests by \((d_i, t_i)\) where \(d_i\) denotes the destination floor.

Let's try to recreate the algorithm that most elevators use. Here's my guess: let's say the elevator is currently going in a direction (either up or down). Then, it will keep a list of pick-up and drop-off floors above and below it at all times. The elevator keeps moving in a given direction until all requests in that direction have been serviced, then it switches directions. Let's call this the "baseline" algorithm.

What are some inefficiencies of this algorithm? First, where the elevator "parks" is important. If most people enter or exit the elevator at the first floor, parking there might make sense. Frustratingly, the elevator in my work building does not exhibit this behavior!

Second, let's take the case of a really tall building, where the elevator is on a downward trajectory from floor \(n-1\) to \(1\). Let's say just as the person on \(n-1\) is picked up and requests floor \(1\), someone on floor \(n\) requests the elevator. Then the elevator will go all the way down to lobby, and leave the person on the top floor waiting for a long time. A better algorithm may have been to go up and get the person on the top floor before beginning its descent.

This, of course, is an edge case. We'll try to iteratively improve on this algorithm throughout the rest of this post.
