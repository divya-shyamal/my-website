The beauty of solitude[1]

Blaise Pascal famously said “All of humanity’s problems stem from man’s inability to sit quietly in a room alone.” Through college, I've come to realize that the most important thing you can do for yourself is learn how to keep yourself fulfilled when you’re alone.

Life, at least as I've lived it, can be broken into three parts. There's the one where you're with your friends or family. It's simply human nature to be fulfilled by their presence. In fact, most of our happiness can be attributed to the quality of our closest relationships (and maybe surprisingly to some, not success). On a different axis, is the time you spend working. For many students, school-related work keeps them occupied for most of the time. If you play your cards right, work can be a source of fulfillment as well. And then, there's there's the third part: the time you spend alone. In my opinion, this is the most important of the three. There is just so much that can be done with this time, because it is so different from the other two. It's when you really figure out who you are, and develop your relationship with yourself.

What makes this time so special is that there's no structure imposed on it at all. You are free to do what you want and think about whatever you want, however you want. The heightened freedom of thought that comes with being alone is something most of us don't realize exists.

When you're with other people, there is structure imposed on thoughts.[2] Conversation itself is structured - it's necessarily a back and forth. You try to find common ground with others, and only say things you think could be interesting or amusing to them. Even in relationships where you feel completely comfortable, it still happens, even if only slightly. We don't blurt out everything we think. We only verbalize, and therefore expand on, the thoughts we see as worth sharing.

As an extreme example, my mind often goes blank in social settings. I never really thought about why this was the case, but it may be because under social pressure, my thoughts become filtered to nothing.

Similarly, when you're working, your thoughts must relate to the task at hand. Any that don't are quickly pushed away as distractions. Due to deadlines and other pressures, even thoughts that are relevant to work can be discarded if they don't seem immediately productive.

You might be thinking: maybe these filters are actually beneficial? Maybe they help us focus on productive thoughts and push away useless ones? But to stumble across our best thoughts, exploration is needed (think \(\epsilon\)-greedy or UCB). Many of us are quick to push away thoughts that don't seem fitting because of the pressures we face, whether in the workplace or in social settings.

This might explain why some of our best thoughts come in the shower. One of the biggest breaks in one of my papers came as my mind wandered there. I hadn't even realized I was thinking about concentration inequalities until later. But that unconstrained exploration was key.

This isn't to say structure on thought is a bad thing. It's just fundamentally different from the unstructured mental space we enter when we're alone.

With this in mind, many of us grossly misuse our alone time. Myself included. Some of us turn back to the things we are relieved from (I know my fair share of work-aholics). Some of us consume articles without truly engaging with them. Some of us try to fill up this time with arrangements with friends. In the worst case, a lot of us turn to our mobile phones. We scroll social media or continue interacting with anyone who bothers to respond to our texts. We seemingly love to impose structure on ourselves, and can't let ourselves sit with ourselves for even a second. However, boredom creates the space for deeper thoughts to naturally emerge, explained by the Default Mode Network in our brains. Instead, we crave interaction for everything and anyone but ourselves. In the famous Electric Shock study, participants chose to administer electric shocks to themselves, rather than just sit peacefully alone in a room for 15 minutes. Any spare time is always filled by whatever our phone has to offer us - not what we have to offer ourselves, which is much richer than most people expect from themselves. This isn't to say you should abandon everything when you have solo time. Just consume in moderation, and engage deeply with whatever you choose to put your mind to.

Psychologist Donald Winnicott states that the capacity to be peacefully alone, and to exist comfortably with your own mind, is a sign of emotional maturity.

So the next time you have a spare moment, just try to sit with your thoughts - whatever comes to mind. And if you don't feel like thinking, do something fulfilling. Cook a meal you've been craving or go to the gym without reaching for your phone between sets. Foster the rich inner world currently unbeknownst to you, and you'll come to take pride in it. This is what makes you who you are - not the prestige of your job, or the material luxuries you can afford.

You'll come to appreciate not only the beauties of what life has to offer, but the beauties of what you have to offer to yourself.
